## Königsaue birch tar

Schmidt and colleagues' [2023 primary study](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s12520-023-01789-2.pdf) analyzed two tar artifacts from Königsaue, Germany, against tars produced by several experimental methods. Their comparative chemical evidence supports an oxygen-restricted, below-ground heating interpretation for these **two pieces**. The authors state that the exact stratigraphic dates remain debated within roughly 45,000–80,000 years before present; the objects are curated at the State Museum of Prehistory in Halle. Their chemical match is an inference about production conditions, not a surviving prehistoric furnace.

Earlier experimental work by Schmidt and colleagues, [PNAS 2019](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/31427508/), showed that usable birch tar can also result from burning bark beside stone in open air. Kozowyk and colleagues' [2017 experimental study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5579016/) compared methods with different yields and material demands. A later [2023 biomolecular pilot](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-023-41898-5) warns that distinguishing precise ancient recipes from chemical markers is difficult. Taken together, these primary studies support a focused question about what the Königsaue comparison can and cannot resolve; they do not establish one universal Neanderthal method or a numerical ranking of cognition.

**Limits:** Reproduction experiments demonstrate possibility under their tested conditions. They cannot recreate ancient labor, instruction, culture or intent by themselves. Avoid the label “primitive humans” for a people and do not infer cognitive inferiority or superiority from one technology. Any field demonstration needs site permission, fire controls, ventilation, material sourcing and qualified supervision; the editorial question is evidence interpretation, not a stepwise build.
