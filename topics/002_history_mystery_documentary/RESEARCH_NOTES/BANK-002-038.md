## Case boundary

Use Piltdown I and II as a single historical case about re-examining specimen provenance, composition and interpretation. Separate the first public claim, contemporary doubts, the 1949–1953 tests, and later forensic work. Present the attribution to Charles Dawson as a research inference, not an established eyewitness fact or a certain account of motive. Do not turn this case into a blanket claim that fossils, museums or evolutionary research are unreliable.

## What the primary reports change

The 1953 authors explicitly say their 1949 fluorine method established that the Piltdown cranium and jaw were **not Lower Pleistocene**, but could not separate Upper Pleistocene from much later material at that site. They also explain that, on an assumption that the pieces were genuine, the early test could appear to support their being contemporary. In the 1953 re-examination, improved fluorine measurement and nitrogen comparisons instead differentiated the jaw and teeth from the cranial pieces. Microscopic scratches, unnaturally flat tooth wear and superficial staining supplied independent lines of evidence. This is a stronger, more accurate narrative than saying one fluorine test instantly proved the hoax. See pp. 141–145 of the [original report](https://archive.org/download/biostor-265698/biostor-265698.pdf).

The 1955 [follow-up](https://archive.org/download/biostor-265700/biostor-265700.pdf?download=1), particularly Oakley's composition chapter at pp. 254–257, makes the cross-check explicit: nitrogen alone can mislead when preservation conditions differ, so the team compared it with fluorine and controls. It says the skull fragments also became suspect as the work proceeded; the earlier interpretation that a genuine skull had simply been paired with a recent ape jaw was not the final one. This helps keep discovery, detection and later reconstruction as separate stages.

The 2016 [full paper](https://www.ebi.ac.uk/europepmc/webservices/rest/PMC5108962/fullTextXML), sections 2–4, reports that its attempted new radiocarbon dates were unsuccessful because samples failed pretesting, showed contamination issues, or yielded no datable material. Its XRF results found iron-rich staining, sometimes with chromium and silver, but the authors could **not** identify a consistent exact stain formula. Shared putty, gravel plugs and tooth modification support a common preparation pattern across both sites; DNA and morphometric results help identify the material used. The authors conclude a single forger was most likely Dawson, while leaving whether anyone assisted him uncertain.

This matters for the niche 039 legacy prompt: its categorical wording about Dawson staining both an orangutan jaw and modern skull with potassium dichromate is not adopted as a verified script claim. The 1953 report documents chromate in particular fragments, and the 2016 study says the exact staining materials remain unidentified. The preserved prompt is an archival record, not a finding.

## Candidate claims and gates

1. **Claim candidate:** The 1949 fluorine test narrowed age but did not settle the jaw/skull association; the 1953 team combined refined chemistry with tooth and staining observations to conclude deliberate forgery. The original 1953 report supports this sequence.
2. **Claim candidate:** The 2016 team inferred consistent preparation across Piltdown I and II and a likely single forger. Its full paper supports that inference; its uncertainty about accomplices and motive must stay visible.
3. **Open:** Check the relevant 1955 specimen tables, methods and individual archive records before quoting a test value or reconstructing a particular collector's actions. Do not equate relative chemical signals with precise absolute age.
4. **Open:** Any broad statement about national motives, institutional bias, peer review, or why particular researchers accepted the finds requires direct historical scholarship and contemporary documents, not merely the later forgery finding.
