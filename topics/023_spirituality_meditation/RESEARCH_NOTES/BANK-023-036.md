This note tests the archived trending pitch `trending_topics/1`, which calls a physiological sigh “the fastest clinically proven breath to stop panic in 30 seconds.” The pitch and its proposed alveoli/vagal explanation are preserved in `local_reconciliation`; they are not verified findings.

## Inspected sources

- Balban et al., [“Brief structured respiration practices enhance mood and reduce physiological arousal,” *Cell Reports Medicine* 4 (2023), 100895](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/36630953/), DOI 10.1016/j.xcrm.2022.100895. The abstract describes a remote randomized comparison of three **daily five-minute** breathing exercises and mindfulness meditation **over one month**. Cyclic sighing emphasized prolonged exhalations. It reports improved mood and reduced respiratory rate relative to mindfulness in the stated mixed-effects analysis and characterizes cyclic sighing as promising for stress management.
- The linked [trial registration, NCT05304000](https://clinicaltrials.gov/study/NCT05304000), identifies the study record. Its outcome details require a separate full-record and protocol inspection before any scripted clinical claim.

## Claim disposition

The inspected abstract supports a bounded description of the intervention and reported group-level outcomes. It does **not** test stopping a diagnosed panic attack in 30 seconds, establish the fastest breathing method, or verify the archived alveoli/vagal mechanism. Anxiety measures in a stress-management study must not be relabeled as acute panic treatment. If this is selected as an episode, inspect the full paper, protocol, participant criteria, outcome tables, adverse events and relevant clinical guidance; compare the exact breathing instruction with any public demonstration.
