# BANK-032-050 — Apollo 11 alarm and descent

**Question:** How did the Apollo 11 crew and ground team decide to continue through the 1201 and 1202 alarms?  
**Review date:** 2026-10-24. **Status:** Bounded premise review. The BANK record remains `proposed`; no script, media clearance or production approval.

## Primary records inspected

1. NASA, [*Apollo 11 Mission Report*](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/apollo50th/pdf/A11_MissionReport.pdf), November 1969, Table 5-I. Its computer-derived event times list a 1202 alarm at mission elapsed time 102:38:22, further 1202 alarms at 102:39:02, 102:42:43 and 102:42:58, a 1201 alarm at 102:42:18, and landing at 102:45:40. These report times should be preferred for exact event chronology over rounded audio transcript timestamps.
2. NASA Apollo 11 Lunar Surface Journal, [“The First Lunar Landing”](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a11/a11.landing.html), edited mission audio transcript and linked technical debrief. At transcript time 102:38:53, CAPCOM tells the crew they are “Go” on the first 1202. At 102:45:02, the “60 seconds” call concerns fuel time until the *Bingo* call, followed by the contact-light report at 102:45:40. This is an annotated archive, and it itself flags differences between transcript and mission-report timing.
3. Fred H. Martin, [“Apollo 11: 25 Years Later”](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/static/history/alsj/a11/a11.1201-fm.html), NASA Lunar Surface Journal, 1994. A guidance-software participant recounts overloaded executive job queues, the rendezvous-radar switch investigation after landing, and priority scheduling that preserved essential tasks. This is a retrospective participant account; the exact software mechanism needs corroboration in contemporary technical records before a script quotes implementation details.

## Claim boundary

- The archived title fuses the **1202 alarm** with the **final 60 seconds**. The first listed 1202 precedes landing by more than seven minutes. The final 1202 is still more than two and a half minutes before landing. The 60-second call came later and referred to the remaining interval until a fuel-reserve decision point, not sixty seconds of vehicle life.
- NASA's report and annotated transcript support repeated alarms, real-time crew/ground evaluation, continued descent, and subsequent fuel calls. They do not support an effortless automatic computer rescue or a single-person decision narrative.
- `BANK-032-033` already covers Apollo navigation and crew–ground coordination broadly. This proposal is the alarm-specific case, including a precise source-timestamp discrepancy. Niche 346 is the home for lunar surface science, not this flight-control decision.
