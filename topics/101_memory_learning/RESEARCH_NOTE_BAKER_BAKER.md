# Bounded source note: name and occupation recall

Accessed 2026-10-24. Primary study: McWeeny, K. H., Young, A. W., Hay, D. C., and Ellis, A. W. (1987), [“Putting names to faces,” *British Journal of Psychology* 78, 143–149](https://bpspsychub.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.2044-8295.1987.tb02235.x), DOI: 10.1111/j.2044-8295.1987.tb02235.x. The publisher’s abstract was accessible; the full article was not read for this note.

Participants learned surnames and occupations paired with 16 unfamiliar faces. The authors report harder recall for names than occupations, including labels that could serve in either role. The abstract says the design reduced some contextual and frequency confounds. This supports a question about how label *role* affected recall under that laboratory task. It does not establish why every real-world name is forgotten, provide a five-second forgetting rate, or test a universal name-memory intervention.

Production follow-up: obtain the full methods and participant details, inspect scoring and effect sizes, and compare later replications before presenting a general claim. The study uses unfamiliar faces and paired labels; its result should not be generalized to clinical memory problems or all social encounters. This is a selected source-access note, not full verification.
