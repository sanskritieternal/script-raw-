# BANK-071-050 — Attention residue and task switching

**Selected question:** What did Leroy's attention-residue experiments actually test?

**Primary source inspection (2026-10-24):** Sophie Leroy, “[Why is it so hard to do my work? The challenge of attention residue when switching between work tasks](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/pii/S0749597809000399),” *Organizational Behavior and Human Decision Processes* 109(2), 168–181 (2009), DOI 10.1016/j.obhdp.2009.04.002. The publisher's abstract describes two experiments on transition between work tasks. It says participants can continue thinking about an unfinished prior task and that subsequent task performance suffers under the tested conditions.

**Related original study:** Leroy and Glomb, “[Tasks Interrupted: How Anticipating Time Pressure on Resumption of an Interrupted Task Causes Attention Residue and Low Performance on Interrupting Tasks and How a ‘Ready-to-Resume’ Plan Mitigates the Effects](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/abs/10.1287/orsc.2017.1184),” *Organization Science* (2018). Its publisher abstract describes an intervention that reduced residue for an interrupting task in the studied setup. It does not establish one universal productivity gain for every workplace.

**Bounded finding:** Attention residue is a task-transition construct with experimental evidence under specified tasks and conditions. The archived “multitasking destroys your IQ” title does not follow from these studies: neither source reports a permanent IQ loss, and the 2009 paper is about carryover and performance on another task. “Deep work” is a broader author framework, not identical to the experiment.

**Access and next checks:** The publisher abstracts and bibliographic records were inspected; full methods, participant counts, instruments and effect sizes were not verified here. Obtain the full papers before quoting numeric effects or writing a causal script. Examine how the experimental tasks transfer to the intended viewer's work.
