## BANK-170-044 - Divorce after age 50

Bowling Green State University's National Center for Family and Marriage Research (NCFMR), [*Marriage Duration at Time of Gray Divorce*](https://www.bgsu.edu/ncfmr/resources/data/family-profiles/FP-24-12.html) (2024), analyzes the 2022 American Community Survey. It reports that the gray-divorce rate rose from 1990 to 2010 but has more recently largely stagnated, with a slight decline for ages 50-64, while the share of divorces involving older adults grew. The same center's [*Age Variation in the Refined Divorce Rate, 1990 & 2023*](https://scholarworks.bgsu.edu/ncfmr_family_profiles/381/) (2025) supplies an age-specific comparison. A share, number and rate have different denominators; none identifies why a specific marriage ended. Geography is the United States and period ends with the cited data.

## BANK-170-045 - Separate-room sleeping

The American Academy of Sleep Medicine's [Sleep Prioritization Survey: Sleep Divorce](https://aasm.org/wp-content/uploads/2023/07/sleep-prioritization-survey-2023-sleep-divorce.pdf) reports a commissioned online survey of 2,005 US adults fielded March 24-29, 2023. Its question asks what respondents do to accommodate a bed partner; the displayed response options include sleeping in another room occasionally and consistently. This is a self-report sample, not a measurement of all happy couples, sleeping in separate bedrooms every night, or causal benefit. The archived 35% figure and "happy couples" framing require a matching measure before use. Survey access or estimates may change; cite the dated instrument.

## BANK-170-046 - Wedding expenses

Andrew Francis-Tan and Hugo M. Mialon, [*"A Diamond Is Forever" and Other Fairy Tales: The Relationship Between Wedding Expenses and Marriage Duration*](https://ideas.repec.org/a/bla/ecinqu/v53y2015i4p1919-1930.html), Economic Inquiry 53 (2015), analyze a survey of more than 3,000 ever-married US respondents. This is observational association, subject to recall, selection, confounding and cohort effects. It does not prove that spending $30,000 causes divorce or that a $500 courthouse ceremony prevents one. The public abstract was checked; obtain full methods and tables before citing subgroup effect sizes or prices.
