This is a bounded source note for a topic proposal, not a researched script. The preserved local queue asks whether Webb “broke the Big Bang.” That is a headline premise, not the result of the inspected study.

## Inspected primary sources

- [Carniani et al., “Spectroscopic confirmation of two luminous galaxies at a redshift of 14,” *Nature* 633 (2024)](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-07860-9) reports JWST/NIRSpec spectra of JADES-GS-z14-0 and JADES-GS-z14-1. The authors fit Lyman-break profiles and report redshift estimates of approximately 14.32 and 13.90, respectively. The paper describes checks on the nearby foreground object's spectral features, contamination and lensing. Its stellar properties, luminosity-function implications and explanations for the observed abundance depend on modeling and survey assumptions; the paper identifies further possibilities to test.
- [NASA's Webb science explainer on galaxies through time](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/webb/science-overview/science-explainers/webb-science-galaxies-through-time/) explicitly distinguishes surprising early-galaxy findings from a disproof of the Big Bang and says the cause of the excess bright galaxies is still being investigated. This is an agency synthesis, not a substitute for the study's methods and uncertainty analysis.

## Claim boundary and next research

The episode can ask how spectroscopy turns a promising imaging candidate into a better constrained redshift and how brightness is translated into a galaxy-population question. It should not treat an estimated stellar mass as a direct measurement, generalize two galaxies to the entire early universe without the selection function, or call a challenge to galaxy-formation expectations a failure of the Big Bang. Before scripting, inspect the study's figures, spectra, uncertainty intervals, selection criteria and subsequent independent observations or competing models. Date every “earliest” or “record” claim because later discoveries can change it.
