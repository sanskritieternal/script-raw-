# BANK-055-052 — Millimeter-scale gravitational clock comparison

**Selected question:** What did the 2022 JILA atomic-clock height comparison show?

**Source inspection (2026-10-24):**

- Bothwell et al., “[Resolving the gravitational redshift across a millimetre-scale atomic sample](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04349-7),” *Nature* 602, 420–424 (published 16 February 2022), DOI 10.1038/s41586-021-04349-7. The authors report a differential frequency measurement across one vertically extended strontium atom sample. The journal page identifies the method, author group and paper version; its data availability statement says underlying experimental data are obtainable from the corresponding authors on reasonable request.
- NIST/JILA, “[JILA Atomic Clocks Measure Einstein’s General Relativity at Millimeter Scale](https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2022/02/jila-atomic-clocks-measure-einsteins-general-relativity-millimeter-scale)” (16 February 2022). The institution describes comparison between upper and lower regions of a *single* roughly 100,000-atom cloud, rather than two separate instruments. It reports a fractional shift on the order of 10⁻¹⁹, consistent with the predicted gravitational redshift.

**Supported bounded premise:** The team resolved a gravitational redshift across roughly a millimeter of vertical separation within an optical lattice clock's atom sample. This is a laboratory frequency comparison under an experimental model. It supports the general direction of gravitational clock-rate difference; the archived “head ages faster than feet” wording is an illustrative extrapolation, not a human age measurement reported by this paper.

**Limits before production:** Inspect the full methods, uncertainty budget and figures before quoting a precise height, frequency ratio or statistical significance. Compare the 2010 NIST two-clock experiment separately if discussing historical firsts. Do not claim this measured a person's age difference or solved quantum gravity.
