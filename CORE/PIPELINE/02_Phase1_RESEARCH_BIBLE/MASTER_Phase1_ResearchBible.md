## RESEARCH PHASE RULES

### Rule 1: Every Significant Claim Needs a Source
- If you cannot find a source, do not state it as fact.
- Mark uncertain claims as "[UNVERIFIED — NEED SOURCE]" and resolve before Phase 2.
- Opinions must be attributed (e.g., "analyst Mark Smith argued...").

### Rule 2: Financial Figures Must Be Verified
- Every dollar amount, percentage, market cap, valuation, and stock price needs at least two credible sources or one primary source (SEC filing, annual report, official company release).
- Round large numbers carefully and state the source year.
- If sources conflict, note the conflict and choose the most credible source with explanation.

### Rule 3: Dates and Timelines Must Be Accurate
- Chronology is critical in documentary storytelling.
- Verify exact dates for: founding, product launches, acquisitions, IPOs, bankruptcies, executive changes, lawsuits, settlements.
- Use a timeline table to cross-check cause-and-effect.

### Rule 4: No Defamation
- Do not state crimes, fraud, or wrongdoing as fact unless there is a conviction, court filing, or credible investigative report.
- Use cautious language: "was accused of," "faced allegations," "was charged with."
- Flag any claim that could expose the project to libel or defamation risk.

### Rule 5: Fair Representation
- If a company is criticized, acknowledge its successes.
- If a founder is praised, acknowledge failures and controversies.
- Include multiple perspectives on controversial topics.

### Rule 6: Distinguish Fact from Speculation
- Speculation must be labeled as such: "Some analysts speculated..." or "The theory suggests..."
- Do not present theories as fact.

## RESEARCH METHODOLOGY

### Step 1: Gather Primary Sources
- SEC filings (10-K, 10-Q, S-1, proxy statements, 8-K) for US public companies
- Company annual reports and investor presentations
- Official press releases and earnings calls
- Court documents, indictments, and regulatory filings
- Government databases (EDGAR, Companies House, etc.)

### Step 2: Cross-Check With Credible Secondary Sources
- Reputable news outlets (Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg, Reuters, CNBC, The Economist)
- Investigative journalism (ProPublica, ICIJ, Reuters Special Reports)
- Academic and business school case studies (Harvard Business Review, Stanford case studies)
- Industry publications (Forbes, Fortune, Business Insider, TechCrunch, The Information)

### Step 3: Verify Financial Numbers
- Use primary source first, then cross-check with 2-3 reputable outlets.
- Note the date of the figure (e.g., "market cap as of December 2024").
- Adjust for inflation when comparing across decades; note the adjustment method.
- Distinguish revenue from profit, valuation from market cap, and stock price from market cap.

### Step 4: Build the Timeline
- List every major event in chronological order.
- For each event: date, what happened, why it matters, source(s).
- Check for logical gaps or contradictions.

### Step 5: Flag Legal and Ethical Risks
- Identify claims that accuse people or companies of wrongdoing.
- Identify claims about living individuals.
- Identify content that could be defamatory, invasive, or unfair.
- Mark risk level: None / Low / Moderate / High / Critical.

### Step 6: Write the Research Summary
- 2-3 page narrative summarizing the story arc.
- Include the central tension, key turning points, and conclusion.
- Reference the primary sources that support the main narrative.

## SOURCE CREDIBILITY GUIDE

### Tier 1 — Primary / Highly Credible
- SEC filings, annual reports, court documents, regulatory filings
- Official company press releases and earnings calls
- Government statistics and central bank reports
- Academic peer-reviewed papers and business school case studies

### Tier 2 — Credible News and Analysis
- Financial Times, Wall Street Journal, Bloomberg, Reuters, The Economist
- CNBC, Fortune, Forbes (investigative pieces, not contributor op-eds)
- ProPublica, ICIJ, Reuters Special Reports
- Harvard Business Review, MIT Sloan Management Review

### Tier 3 — Reputable But Verify
- Business Insider, TechCrunch, The Information, Axios
- Wired, Fast Company, Inc.
- Specialist industry publications (CoinDesk, Chainalysis, etc.)

### Tier 4 — Use With Caution
- Contributor op-eds, anonymous blogs, social media posts
- Unverified leaks, rumors, conspiracy theories
- AI-generated content without human editorial oversight
- Content farms and sponsored content without disclosure

### Tier 5 — Do Not Use
- Tabloid sensationalism, unverified claims, hate sites
- Sources with undisclosed conflicts of interest
- Content that is itself the subject of legal disputes

## FINANCIAL VERIFICATION PROTOCOL

### Critical Finance Facts to Verify

**Company Financials:**
- [ ] Revenue figures (check annual reports/SEC filings)
- [ ] Profit/loss (not just revenue)
- [ ] Market capitalization (actual, not estimated)
- [ ] Valuation (how was it calculated?)
- [ ] Stock price (actual historical price)

**Billionaire Net Worth:**
- [ ] Verified against Forbes/Bloomberg
- [ ] Include date (net worth changes constantly)
- [ ] Note: estimates vary by source
- [ ] Disclose if estimates or official

**Deal Values:**
- [ ] Acquisition price (exactly how much?)
- [ ] Stock or cash (different implications)
- [ ] Date of deal
- [ ] Any conditions (earnouts, etc.)

**Startup Valuations:**
- [ ] Funding round amount
- [ ] Valuation at that round
- [ ] Series (A, B, C, etc.)
- [ ] Investor names
- [ ] Date of funding

**Market Data:**
- [ ] Stock prices (specific date noted)
- [ ] Market capitalization (at what date?)
- [ ] Trading volume (where applicable)
- [ ] Market indices (S&P 500, etc.)

### Verification Process

**Step 1: Find Primary Source**
- SEC EDGAR (US public companies)
- Company annual reports
- Press releases (official announcements)
- Official financial statements
- Government filings

**Step 2: Cross-Check Multiple Sources**
- At least 2-3 reputable sources
- Note if sources disagree (explain why)
- Use most recent data
- Check for corrections/updates to earlier claims

**Step 3: Adjust for Context**
- Inflation adjustments (1980s dollars ≠ 2024 dollars)
- One-time vs recurring revenue
- Extraordinary items (one-time losses)
- Stock splits (affects historical prices)

**Step 4: Document Sources**
- List actual sources in description
- Include links where possible
- Note date of information
- Update if numbers change

## LEGAL LIABILITY ASSESSMENT

### High-Risk Content (Legal Review Recommended)

**Red Flags:**
- Directly accusing company/person of crime
- Claiming fraud without evidence
- Alleging health dangers from product
- Making defamatory statements
- Conspiracy theories presented as fact

### When to Get Legal Review
- If accusing named person/company of wrongdoing
- If claiming product is dangerous
- If alleging fraud or criminal behavior
- If controversial and large audience
- If sponsored (liability on sponsor too)

### Language to Avoid

**Don't Say:**
- "[Company] is a scam" (accusation without evidence)
- "[CEO] is corrupt" (libel if false)
- "This product causes cancer" (claim without proof)
- "[Company] broke the law" (claim without conviction)

**Do Say:**
- "[Company] faced fraud accusations"
- "[CEO] was charged with..." (state facts)
- "Some people reported [health issue] after using product"
- "[Company] faced legal issues including..."

### Defamation Liability Check
- [ ] False statement of fact (not opinion)
- [ ] Causes harm to reputation
- [ ] Communicated to others
- [ ] Creator was negligent/intentional
- [ ] Actual damages

If any of these are present, flag for legal review.

## BIAS DETECTION & FAIR REPRESENTATION

### Bias Checklist
- [ ] If criticizing a company, did you acknowledge its successes?
- [ ] If praising a founder, did you acknowledge failures/controversies?
- [ ] Are multiple perspectives shown on controversial topics?
- [ ] Is there unfair advantage given to competitors?
- [ ] Are conflicts of interest disclosed?
- [ ] Are sources diverse in perspective?
- [ ] Are emotionally loaded words minimized in favor of facts?

### Common Bias Traps
- **Hero worship:** Portraying founders as geniuses without flaws
- **Villain framing:** Portraying failed companies as purely evil
- **Presentism:** Judging past decisions with today's knowledge
- **Single-cause fallacy:** Saying "X caused Y" when multiple factors were involved
- **Cherry-picking:** Selecting only data that supports the narrative

### Balance Note
The subject is complex. Most failures involve market forces, timing, competition, and luck — not just bad decisions. Most successes involve luck, timing, and team effort — not just genius. Reflect that complexity.
